[8] STYLE='Normal'                     | AbstractΓÇöThe result of large language model is fluent text that might contain statements that contradict the or are not consistent with the existing evidence, and the general evaluation of a response might mask such flaws in terms of otherwise correct statements. In our work, we present. The proposed approach called VERIFACT consists of the following steps: claim segmentation, evidence retrieval per claim with the aid of a dense bi-encoder; re-ranking of candidates using cross-encoder; labeling of each claim-evidence pair as supported, refuted, or not enough information with the aid of fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-small model; post-processing of the results with the help of temperature scaling; and calculation of factuality score from evidence retrieved from the top three passages. The pipeline was developed based on hierarchical data with 15 different topics with 308-passage retrieval corpus (which is an extension of 302 with six human-verified ground-truth passages P0303-P0308) and 200 responses in the heldout test set. With a balanced validation set of 105 claims, the verifier provides 89.52% (0.8958) accuracy (macro-F1), while temperature scaling (╧ä = 1.1348) reduces the expected calibration error from 0.0810 to 0.0721. For 50 queries in the small corpus, recall@5 equals 1.000, and claim extraction provides F1 = 0.9767 on 22 claims. In the held-out benchmark, the end-to-end accuracy is 50.0%, and 33 out of 52 errors are caused by evidence retrieval

[11] STYLE='Normal'                     | ARLMs are trained to assign high probabilities to likely continuations, not to verify the truthfulness of the continuation itself. Inevitably, it results in fluent output which states things that are contrary to reality or facts, termed as hallucinations [1]. Depending on the context, it can lead to a dosage mismatch in the summary of a medical report, a nonexistent citation in the legal report, or a fabricated date in a news article ΓÇô all passing a cursory look but failing verification from the sources.
[12] STYLE='Normal'                     | Checking a response against a reference as one unit fits this problem poorly, because a mostly correct response can contain a single false sentence that an aggregate judgment absorbs. Splitting long-form text into atomic facts and scoring each against a knowledge source has been proposed for generation quality[2], and evidence-based claim verification, in which a claim is labeled supported, refuted or lacking enough information, was formalized earlier by the FEVER task [3]. A practical verifier needs both: decomposition to localize errors, and retrieval plus inference to judge each piece.
[13] STYLE='Normal'                     | Building such a verifier from small, locally runnable components exposes several recurring difficulties. A response mixes verifiable statements with opinions, advice and transitions, so claims must be isolated before they are checked. Retrieval that rewards topical or lexical similarity can return a passage that mentions the same entities as a false claim without bearing on its truth. The confidence scores of a trained classifier cannot be assumed to match its accuracy [4]. Finally, claimΓÇôevidence data built from templates can carry surface cues that allow a classifier to succeed without reading the evidence [5], which makes evaluation on such data optimistic.

Work done in term of the issue of factual correctness in respect of summarization has shown that NLI systems can be used as a verdict platform [9]. Certain instances of that technology are based on many runs of a large language model, which is expensive and complicates local reproduction.
[25] STYLE='Heading 2'                  | C. Retrieval and Reranking
[26] STYLE='Normal'                     | Sparse methods such as BM25 and TF-IDF match shared terms and miss paraphrases, which dense retrievers address by embedding queries and passages in one vector space [10]. A bi-encoder encodes the two sides separately and so cannot model token-level interaction between claim and passage; cross-encoders that read both together rank better and are commonly applied to a short candidate list [11], trained on data such as MS MACRO. Distilled encoders such as MiniLM keep both stages inexpensive, and approximate nearest-neighbor search over HNSW graphs keeps lookup fast.
[27] STYLE='Heading 2'                  | D. Natural Language Inference and Calibration
[28] STYLE='Normal'                     | NLI corpora such as MNLI underpin most verdict classifiers. Pretrained encoders such as BERT and RoBERTa [12] raised NLI accuracy, and DeBERTa keeps content and relative position as separate representations inside attention; its third version moves pretraining to replaced-token detection with gradient-disentangled embedding sharing [13]. Annotation artifacts show that high benchmark accuracy can come from cues in the hypothesis alone [5]. For calibration, temperature scaling rescales logits with one learned scalar and was found effective for deep networks [4]; for pretrained transformers, in-domain calibration is already reasonable before correction and temperature scaling lowers the remaining error [14]. Calibration error is usually summarized by binned expected calibration error [15].

[43] STYLE='Heading 2'                  | B. Claim Extraction
[44] STYLE='Normal'                     | Raw model output interleaves checkable statements with hedges and discourse, so extraction is rule-based and runs in four steps. Normalization removes quotation marks, escaped apostrophes and typographic artifacts that disturb sentence splitting. A regular-expression splitter then separates sentences at terminal punctuation and line breaks. Sentences that match opinion patterns (for example ΓÇ£I thinkΓÇ¥ or ΓÇ£many people believeΓÇ¥), questions, and sentences shorter than five tokens are discarded. The remaining sentences are kept if they contain a four-digit year, a quantity, a copular verb or a definite action predicate such as invented, founded or published. If no sentence passes, declarative sentences of at least three words are used so that the interface never returns an empty result. Each retained claim is assigned by pattern matching to one of 11 categories: person, date, number, location, organization, event, definition, scientific, comparison, causeΓÇôeffect and relationship.

[50] STYLE='Normal'                     | Semantic Relevance Gate. After the cross-encoder reranking phase but before going through the NLI process, VERIFACT uses a Semantic Relevance Gate. MS-MARCO cross-encoder (ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2) provides each candidate retrieval with a log-likelihood logit score. If a candidate retrieval has logit scores below the threshold value of ╧ä = ΓêÆ2.5, then the candidate is considered to be irrelevant to the claim. [51] STYLE='Heading 2'                  | E. NLI Verifier
[52] STYLE='Normal'                     | The verifier adopts a dual-engine approach. In the case of the default setting, the pre-trained RoBERTa-large-MNLI model [12] , trained on over 400,000 MultiNLI inference pairs, is employed for robust generalization towards open-world queries. For the benchmarking mode, the fine-tuned microsoft/deberta-v3-small [13] model, trained on the VERIFACT 240-sample NLI dataset, is utilized. The particular engine can be chosen in the Streamlit sidebar at runtime. Claim and evidence are concatenated as ΓÇ£Claim: <c> [SEP] Evidence: <e>ΓÇ¥ with a maximum length of 256 tokens; this pattern is chosen among five possible candidates (VI-B). Disentangled attention enables the model to have separate content and relative-position vectors; therefore, it is suitable for situations involving negation, word ordering, or entity roles[13].

[58] STYLE='Heading 2'                  | G. Multi-Evidence Aggregation and Response Verdict
[59] STYLE='Normal'                     | Depending on the first passage alone could lead to misclassification of the claim because a somewhat relevant top passage could give the wrong class while the second and third contain the key evidence needed to label the claim. Post-relevance gating (IV-DΓÇÖ), VERIFACT classifies the top-k (Γëñ3) most semantically relevant candidates by using the NLI module based on a Contradiction-Precedence Aggregation rule: (1) the claim is classified SUPPORTS if at least one candidate returns a SUPPORTS label with confidence ΓëÑ0.45 and no candidate returns REFUTES; (2) the claim is classified REFUTES if at least one candidate returns REFUTES with confidence ΓëÑ0.60 regardless of support signals; and (3) when both are present but REFUTES < 0.60, the most confident support verdict overrides all. The aggregator uses three response level rules in order to classify the top three passages based on the calibrated predictions. (1) If any passage returns P(REFUTES) ΓëÑ 0.60, the claim is REFUTES such that noisy evidence cannot override a hallucination. (2) Otherwise, if any passage returns P(SUPPORTS) ΓëÑ 0.60, the claim is SUPPORTS. (3) Otherwise, the claim is NOT_ENOUGH_INFO. Claim verdicts are converted to response labels using F(X), nsup, nref and m (Table I).

[109] STYLE='Heading 1'                  | VII. Discussion
[110] STYLE='Normal'                     | The main observation is the distance between component-level and end-to-end figures. The verifier classifies 89.52% of validation pairs correctly, retrieval ranks the gold passage first for all 50 test queries, and extraction misses one of 22 claims, yet the assembled pipeline reaches 0.500 end-to-end accuracy. The component results come from small curated sets, whereas the end-to-end benchmark involves 4,550 candidate passages and un-curated responses. The error log fits retrieval being where this difference matters, since 33 of 52 errors involved a passage on the same entity or region but not on the claim. This is a reading of the log, not a tested causal result; supplying gold evidence to the verifier on the held-out benchmark would separate retrieval errors from verification errors.
[111] STYLE='Normal'                     | Among the development steps, hard contrastive negatives gave the largest single macro-F1 gain, followed by cleaned data and class weighting. The weighting result matches its motivation, because refuted claims in this data share much of their vocabulary with true ones, and REFUTES F1 rose from 0.5230 to 0.6750 across the first four stages. Calibration changed the picture little: the ECE reduction of 0.0089 and Brier reduction of 0.0005 agree with reports that pretrained transformers are already reasonably calibrated in-domain [14]. With 105 items over ten bins, and ╧ä fitted on the same split, the ECE estimate is noisy and probably optimistic, so these figures show that scaling did no harm rather than that confidence can be trusted.
[112] STYLE='Normal'                     | The refutation-first rule is a deliberate trade. One passage out of three with P(REFUTES) ΓëÑ 0.60 overrides supportive evidence, which favors sensitivity to hallucinations and accepts a higher risk of false refutations, as in the double-helix example. The 0.60 threshold and the rule itself were not ablated, so their effect on the balance between false refutations and missed hallucinations is not quantified.

[114] STYLE='Heading 1'                  | VIII. Limitations
[115] STYLE='List Paragraph'             | Scale. The validation split has 105 claims, the retrieval test 50 queries and the extraction audit 22 claims. Differences of a few points lie within what a handful of examples can produce, and no confidence intervals are reported.
[116] STYLE='List Paragraph'             | Selection and reporting. Hyperparameters and ╧ä were chosen on the validation split, where the NLI and calibration results are also reported. Results on a Layer 1 test split are not reported, so the NLI figures are likely optimistic.
[117] STYLE='List Paragraph'             | Ablation design. The rows of Table VII differ in more than one respect, and the M4ΓÇôM5 and M5ΓÇôM6 changes cannot be assigned to the components their labels name.
[118] STYLE='List Paragraph'             | Closed corpus. Data is extracted from 308 sentences where at most three sentences are evaluated for each claim, which means claims verified using data from outside the corpus or via multiple hops are beyond scope.
[119] STYLE='List Paragraph'             | Extraction. Extraction is rule-based and was scored on 22 claims; handling of abbreviations and quoted text was not measured.
[120] STYLE='List Paragraph'             | Data construction. The absence of label leakage rests on how the data were built; a quantitative check, such as a claim-only baseline, and annotation agreement are not reported.
[121] STYLE='List Paragraph'             | Decision rules. The criteria in Table I overlap in places and do not cover a response in which every claim is NOT_ENOUGH_INFO.

[123] STYLE='Heading 1'                  | IX. Conclusion and Future Work
[124] STYLE='Normal'                     | VERIFACT was proposed in this paper as an AI generated claim level verification pipeline using rule based extraction, dense retrieval followed by cross-encoder re-ranking and NLI verifier which is a combination of two engines (open domain: RoBERTa-large-MNLI; benchmark domain: fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-small) with temperature scaling, a semantic relevance filter with (╧ä=ΓêÆ2.5) and a contradiction precedence aggregator. The verifier got validation accuracy of 89.52% with calibration error of 0.0810 reduced to 0.0721, but overall accuracy of end-to-end model on the held-out benchmark was 50.0%, with wrong evidence retrieval being the top logged error. Thus, the evaluation concludes with more conservative conclusions than the scores suggest, that with small models and curated evidence sets, having a good verifier does not make the pipeline reliable.
[125] STYLE='Normal'                     | Future work follows from the limitations: running the verifier with gold evidence to isolate retrieval from verification errors; reporting held-out test metrics with confidence intervals; ablating the aggregation rule and its threshold; adding entity-aware handling to the reranking stage; expanding the corpus and adding live web retrieval; supporting multi-hop evidence; distilling the verifier into a model small enough for on-device use; and extending verification to multimodal claims.
