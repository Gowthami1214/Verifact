[0] VERIFACT: VERIfication of FACTual Assertions via Calibrated Transducers
[3] AbstractΓÇöThe result of large language model is fluent text that might contain statements that contradict the or are not consistent with the existing evidence, and the general evaluation of a response might mask such flaws in terms of otherwise correct statements. In our work, we present TruthSync, a six-step pipeline that includes: breaking up the response into claims; evidence retrieval for each claim with the help of a dense bi-encoder; re-ranking of the candidates using a cross-encoder; label assignment for each claim-evidence pair as supported, refuted, or not enough information using a fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-small model; post-processing of the results with the help of temperature scaling; and calculation of factuality score from the evidence from top three passages. The pipeline was built on the basis of a hierarchical dataset containing 15 different topics, with the 302-passage retrieval corpus and the held-out test set including 200 responses. For a balanced validation set of 105 claims, the verifier achieves accuracy (macro-F1) of 89.52% (0.8958), while temperature scaling (╧ä = 1.1348) decreases expected calibration error from 0.0810 to 0.0721. On 50 queries for a small corpus, recall at 5 is 1.000, and claim extraction achieves F1 = 0.9767 on 22 claims. On the held-out benchmark, the end-to-end accuracy is 50.0%, and 33 out of 52 mistakes are related to evidence retrieval.

[5] I. Introduction
[6] ARLMs are trained to assign high probabilities to likely continuations, not to verify the truthfulness of the continuation itself. Inevitably, it results in fluent output which states things that are contrary to reality or facts, termed as hallucinations [1], [2]. Depending on the context, it can lead to a dosage mismatch in the summary of a medical report, a nonexistent citation in the legal report, or a fabricated date in a news article ΓÇô all passing a cursory look but failing verification from the sources.
[7] Checking a response against a reference as one unit fits this problem poorly, because a mostly correct response can contain a single false sentence that an aggregate judgment absorbs. Splitting long-form text into atomic facts and scoring each against a knowledge source has been proposed for generation quality [3], and evidence-based claim verification, in which a claim is labeled supported, refuted or lacking enough information, was formalized earlier by the FEVER task [4]. A practical verifier needs both: decomposition to localize errors, and retrieval plus inference to judge each piece.
[8] Building such a verifier from small, locally runnable components exposes several recurring difficulties. A response mixes verifiable statements with opinions, advice and transitions, so claims must be isolated before they are checked. Retrieval that rewards topical or lexical similarity can return a passage that mentions the same entities as a false claim without bearing on its truth. The confidence scores of a trained classifier cannot be assumed to match its accuracy [5]. Finally, claimΓÇôevidence data built from templates can carry surface cues that allow a classifier to succeed without reading the evidence [6], which makes evaluation on such data optimistic.
[9] This paper describes TruthSync, a six-stage pipeline built around these points, and reports how each stage and the assembled pipeline perform on data constructed to avoid prompt-artifact cues. The contributions are:
[10] A claim-level verification pipeline combining rule-based claim extraction, two-stage retrieval, a fine-tuned NLI verifier, temperature scaling and a refutation-first aggregation rule, which yields per-claim verdicts and a response-level factuality score.
[11] A layered 15-domain dataset with a 302-passage retrieval corpus and a separate held-out benchmark of 200 responses containing 910 claims, constructed without prompt-artifact tokens.
[12] A study of the design choices varied during development: input template, loss weighting, hard contrastive negatives, calibration and the final multi-evidence configuration.
[13] An error analysis showing that a verifier validation accuracy of 89.52% sits alongside 50.0% end-to-end accuracy on the held-out benchmark, with retrieval of wrong evidence the most frequent logged error.
[14] Section II reviews related work and states the research gap, Section III formalizes the task, Section IV describes the framework, Section V gives the experimental protocol, Section VI reports results, and Sections VIIΓÇôIX cover discussion, limitations and conclusions.
[15] II. Related Work
[16] A. Claim Verification Datasets and Pipelines
[17] FEVER cast verification as retrieving Wikipedia evidence and assigning one of three labels [4]. SciFact moved the task to scientific claims that need specialist reasoning [7], and VitaminC paired claims with minimally edited evidence so that models could not rely on lexical shortcuts [8]. A survey of the area organizes automated fact-checking as claim detection, evidence retrieval, verdict prediction and justification production [9]; the pipeline studied here follows the same sequence and adds calibration and response-level scoring.
[18] B. Hallucination Detection in Generated Text
[19] Surveys of hallucination separate content that contradicts the source from content that cannot be verified from it [1], [2]. Detection methods differ mainly in where evidence comes from. SelfCheckGPT compares several sampled responses and flags statements on which they disagree, which needs no external knowledge but depends on repeated generation [10]. FActScore reports the share of atomic facts in a text that a knowledge source supports [3]. FacTool and SAFE use a language model to organize evidence gathering from external tools or web search for individual claims [11], [12], and RARR retrieves evidence in order to revise unsupported statements [13]. Work on factual consistency in summarization showed that NLI models can serve as the verdict component [14], and the TRUE benchmark compares such metrics across tasks [15]. Some of these systems depend on repeated calls to a large language model, which raises cost and complicates local reproduction.
[20] C. Retrieval and Reranking
[21] Sparse methods such as BM25 and TF-IDF match shared terms and miss paraphrases, which dense retrievers address by embedding queries and passages in one vector space [16], [17]. A bi-encoder encodes the two sides separately and so cannot model token-level interaction between claim and passage; cross-encoders that read both together rank better and are commonly applied to a short candidate list [18], trained on data such as MS MARCO [19]. Distilled encoders such as MiniLM [20] keep both stages inexpensive, and approximate nearest-neighbor search over HNSW graphs [21] keeps lookup fast.
[22] D. Natural Language Inference and Calibration
[23] NLI corpora such as MNLI [22] underpin most verdict classifiers. Pretrained encoders such as BERT [23] and RoBERTa [24] raised NLI accuracy, and DeBERTa keeps content and relative position as separate representations inside attention [25]; its third version moves pretraining to replaced-token detection with gradient-disentangled embedding sharing [26]. Annotation artifacts show that high benchmark accuracy can come from cues in the hypothesis alone [6]. For calibration, temperature scaling rescales logits with one learned scalar and was found effective for deep networks [5]; for pretrained transformers, in-domain calibration is already reasonable before correction and temperature scaling lowers the remaining error [27]. Calibration error is usually summarized by binned expected calibration error [28].
[24] E. Research Gap and Objectives
[25] The literature supplies mature components for each step but says less about how they behave when chained. Evaluations often examine one stage in isolation, such as retrieval recall or NLI accuracy, or rely on a large language model for the verdict; comparatively little is reported on how stage-level scores of a compact pipeline of small models relate to end-to-end accuracy on responses that mix claims, opinions and distractor evidence. The review here was targeted rather than systematic, so the gap is stated for the studies discussed.
[26] The objectives are therefore (O1) to assemble a calibrated claim-level pipeline from small models suited to CPU inference; (O2) to evaluate each stage and the full pipeline on data built to avoid prompt-artifact cues; (O3) to measure the effect of the main design choices; and (O4) to characterize the errors that remain.
[27] III. Problem Formulation
[28] Let X be a text produced by a generative model. A claim extraction operator E maps X to a set C = {c1, ΓÇª, cm} of atomic, independently checkable statements. Given a reference corpus K, a retrieval function returns k candidate passages for each claim, and a cross-encoder score S(ci, p) orders them. A verifier maps a claim and one passage to a logit vector z = [zsup, zref, znei], and a temperature ╧ä > 0 gives calibrated class probabilities

[33] IV. The TruthSync Framework
[34] A. Overview
[35] Fig. 1 shows the data flow. A response is normalized and split into claims; each claim is embedded and matched against an indexed corpus; the ten nearest passages are reranked and the top three go to the verifier; the verifier logits are rescaled by temperature; and an aggregation rule turns the three passage-level predictions into one verdict per claim. Claim verdicts are then combined into the response score.
[37] Fig. 1.  Overall architecture of the TruthSync pipeline.
[38] B. Claim Extraction
[39] Raw model output interleaves checkable statements with hedges and discourse, so extraction is rule-based and runs in four steps. Normalization removes quotation marks, escaped apostrophes and typographic artifacts that disturb sentence splitting. A regular-expression splitter then separates sentences at terminal punctuation and line breaks. Sentences that match opinion patterns (for example ΓÇ£I thinkΓÇ¥ or ΓÇ£many people believeΓÇ¥), questions, and sentences shorter than five tokens are discarded. The remaining sentences are kept if they contain a four-digit year, a quantity, a copular verb or a definite action predicate such as invented, founded or published. If no sentence passes, declarative sentences of at least three words are used so that the interface never returns an empty result. Each retained claim is assigned by pattern matching to one of 11 categories: person, date, number, location, organization, event, definition, scientific, comparison, causeΓÇôeffect and relationship.
[40] C. Dense Retrieval
[41] The knowledge store K holds 302 curated passages on 15 topics. Each passage and claim is encoded by the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence encoder [17], [20] into a 384-dimensional vector by mean pooling, and the vectors are indexed in ChromaDB with an HNSW graph [21]. For a claim ci the engine returns the k = 10 passages with the highest cosine similarity,

[43] D. Cross-Encoder Reranking
[44] A bi-encoder can rank a passage highly because it shares keywords with a claim. A claim placing the US Constitution in Antarctica, for instance, may pull a passage about Antarctic exploration. The second stage therefore applies the cross-encoder ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 [18], [19], which reads the sequence [CLS] claim [SEP] passage [SEP] through six transformer layers and projects the [CLS] representation to a scalar relevance score. Only the top k* = 3 passages are forwarded to the verifier.
[45] E. NLI Verifier
[46] The verifier is microsoft/deberta-v3-small [26] fine-tuned for three-way classification. The claim and the passage are joined as ΓÇ£Claim: <c> [SEP] Evidence: <e>ΓÇ¥ with a maximum length of 256 tokens; this template was chosen from five candidates (Section VI-B). Disentangled attention gives the model separate content and relative-position vectors, which suits cases that depend on negation, word order or entity roles [25].

[52] G. Multi-Evidence Aggregation and Response Verdict
[53] Trusting only the first passage is risky, since a partly relevant top passage can yield a wrong label while the second or third holds decisive evidence. The aggregator therefore applies three rules to the calibrated predictions for the top three passages. (1) If any passage gives P(REFUTES) ΓëÑ 0.60, the claim is REFUTES, so that noisy or conflicting evidence cannot mask a hallucination. (2) Otherwise, if any passage gives P(SUPPORTS) ΓëÑ 0.60, the claim is SUPPORTS. (3) Otherwise the claim is NOT_ENOUGH_INFO. Claim verdicts are mapped to five response-level labels using F(X), the number of supported claims nsup, the number of refuted claims nref and the claim count m (Table I).

[57] H. Interface
[58] The pipeline is exposed through a Gradio application and a Streamlit dashboard. The Gradio interface shows color-coded verdict banners with a factuality meter, a per-claim breakdown and a JSON diagnostic payload, and its clear action resets input, verdict and payload together so that no stale result remains. The Streamlit dashboard adds sidebar parameter controls and counters for supported and refuted claims.
[59] V. Experimental Setup
[60] A. Data
[61] Data are organized in six layers (Table II) spanning 15 topic domains: science, technology, history, geography, politics and government, economics, sports, medicine and health, environment, education, space, companies and organizations, famous people, culture, and general knowledge. Layer 0 is the FEVER training data [4], used to adapt the NLI backbone before task fine-tuning. Layer 1 holds 300 domain claims with balanced classes and no prompt-artifact tokens, and its validation split contains 105 claims, 35 per class. Layer 2 supplies the 302-passage corpus and the retrieval test pairs. Layer 3 provides sentence-level annotations for claim extraction, Layer 4 a 15-document integration check, and Layer 5 the held-out end-to-end benchmark of 200 responses, 910 claims and 4,550 candidate passages, which the data description states has no overlap with training data.

[92] Table VII and Fig. 2 trace six configurations. Validation macro-F1 rises from 0.3732 to 0.5620 over the first five (+0.1888) and then to 0.8958 for the final configuration (+0.3338). [99] E. End-to-End Behavior and Error Analysis
[100] On the held-out benchmark the full pipeline reached an end-to-end accuracy of 0.500, against 0.467 for the preceding configuration. The error log records 52 misclassified cases ([CONFIRM: evaluation set from which the log was drawn]), which were grouped into five failure modes (Fig. 3). Wrong evidence retrieval is the largest group (33 cases, 63.5%): a keyword shared with the claim, such as a place name, pulls a passage on an unrelated subject, for example a claim that public-key cryptography is located in Madagascar matched to a passage on distributed ledgers. Entity confusion follows (8 cases, 15.4%), as when a correct association of the double helix with Watson and Crick was labeled REFUTES. The remainder are numerical and unit mismatches (5 cases, 9.6%), temporal ambiguity between creation and standardization dates (3 cases, 5.8%) and lexical-overlap traps (3 cases, 5.8%).

[103] VII. Discussion
[104] The main observation is the distance between component-level and end-to-end figures. The verifier classifies 89.52% of validation pairs correctly, retrieval ranks the gold passage first for all 50 test queries, and extraction misses one of 22 claims, yet the assembled pipeline reaches 0.500 end-to-end accuracy. The component results come from small curated sets, whereas the end-to-end benchmark involves 4,550 candidate passages and un-curated responses. The error log fits retrieval being where this difference matters, since 33 of 52 errors involved a passage on the same entity or region but not on the claim. This is a reading of the log, not a tested causal result; supplying gold evidence to the verifier on the held-out benchmark would separate retrieval errors from verification errors.
[105] Among the development steps, hard contrastive negatives gave the largest single macro-F1 gain, followed by cleaned data and class weighting. The weighting result matches its motivation, because refuted claims in this data share much of their vocabulary with true ones, and REFUTES F1 rose from 0.5230 to 0.6750 across the first four stages. Calibration changed the picture little: the ECE reduction of 0.0089 and Brier reduction of 0.0005 agree with reports that pretrained transformers are already reasonably calibrated in-domain [27]. With 105 items over ten bins, and ╧ä fitted on the same split, the ECE estimate is noisy and probably optimistic, so these figures show that scaling did no harm rather than that confidence can be trusted.
[106] The refutation-first rule is a deliberate trade. One passage out of three with P(REFUTES) ΓëÑ 0.60 overrides supportive evidence, which favors sensitivity to hallucinations and accepts a higher risk of false refutations, as in the double-helix example. The 0.60 threshold and the rule itself were not ablated, so their effect on the balance between false refutations and missed hallucinations is not quantified.
[107] No external system was run on this benchmark, so the paper makes no claim of superiority over published fact-checking or hallucination-detection methods. Perfect Recall@k on a 302-passage pool is not comparable with open-domain retrieval results on Wikipedia-scale corpora.
[108] VIII. Limitations
[109] Scale. The validation split has 105 claims, the retrieval test 50 queries and the extraction audit 22 claims. Differences of a few points lie within what a handful of examples can produce, and no confidence intervals are reported.
[110] Selection and reporting. Hyperparameters and ╧ä were chosen on the validation split, where the NLI and calibration results are also reported. Results on a Layer 1 test split are not reported, so the NLI figures are likely optimistic.
[111] Ablation design. The rows of Table VII differ in more than one respect, and the M4ΓÇôM5 and M5ΓÇôM6 changes cannot be assigned to the components their labels name.
[112] Closed corpus. Evidence comes from 302 passages and at most three are checked per claim, so claims supported outside the pool or needing multi-hop synthesis are out of scope.
[113] Extraction. Extraction is rule-based and was scored on 22 claims; handling of abbreviations and quoted text was not measured.
[114] Data construction. The absence of label leakage rests on how the data were built; a quantitative check, such as a claim-only baseline, and annotation agreement are not reported.
[115] Decision rules. The criteria in Table I overlap in places and do not cover a response in which every claim is NOT_ENOUGH_INFO.
[116] Baselines and hardware. No baseline systems were run, and the latency of about 120 ms per claim for the verifier on CPU is stated without hardware details.
[117] IX. Conclusion and Future Work
[118] This paper presented TruthSync, a claim-level pipeline for verifying AI-generated text that joins rule-based extraction, dense retrieval with cross-encoder reranking, a fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-small verifier, temperature scaling and a refutation-first aggregator. The verifier reached 89.52% validation accuracy and calibration error fell from 0.0810 to 0.0721, but end-to-end accuracy on the held-out benchmark was 50.0%, and wrong-evidence retrieval was the most frequent logged error. The evaluation therefore supports a narrower conclusion than component scores alone suggest: with small models on curated data, a strong verifier does not guarantee a reliable pipeline unless retrieval holds up on larger, noisier evidence pools.
[119] Future work follows from the limitations: running the verifier with gold evidence to isolate retrieval from verification errors; reporting held-out test metrics with confidence intervals; ablating the aggregation rule and its threshold; adding entity-aware handling to the reranking stage; expanding the corpus and adding live web retrieval; supporting multi-hop evidence; distilling the verifier into a model small enough for on-device use; and extending verification to multimodal claims.
